A graph can have a perfectly good limit and still be broken at one point. Here's how to tell the difference.

Continuity Detective

Not every strange graph is broken in the same way. Sometimes the function has a hole, sometimes it jumps, and sometimes it shoots off to infinity. These are all different kinds of discontinuities.

Imagine three real situations:

  • A smooth bike path that never changes unexpectedly.
  • An elevator that suddenly jumps from the 2nd floor to the 7th floor.
  • A bridge with a missing plank that makes the path disappear for a moment.

Which of these is continuous all the way through?



Three Kinds of Breaks

Here are three common "breaks" you can spot in a graph:

  • Removable discontinuity: a hole—like a missing tile on the floor.
  • Jump discontinuity: the graph leaps from one height to another.
  • Infinite discontinuity: the graph shoots up or down dramatically.

Think of a video game character walking across a platform. If the platform suddenly vanishes for an instant, then reappears, that is a strange kind of gap.

Which kind of break is most like a hole in the graph?



Rediscovering Continuity

Here's the key puzzle: what must be true for a graph to feel smooth and connected at a point?

At $x=a$, a function is continuous if the value of the function and the nearby trend line up perfectly.

Select all the ingredients that must be true for continuity at $x=a$.




$$f \text{ is continuous at } x=a \iff \lim_{x \to a} f(x)=f(a)$$

You just rediscovered the definition of continuity.

In plain English: the graph must not "jump," "tear," or "vanish" at that point. The nearby behavior and the exact value have to agree.

That is why a hole can be a limit problem, but a true discontinuity is a continuity problem.
